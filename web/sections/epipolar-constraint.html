The Epipolar Constraint

Notation and Setup

Given two cameras with camera projection centers \(C\) and \(C'\), a 3D world point \[ \tilde{P} = \begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \] is projected onto their corresponding image planes at the image points \(\tilde{p}_I\) and \(\tilde{p}_I'\). Each image plane has a principal point, denoted by \((c_x,c_y)\) and \((c_x',c_y')\). The principal point is the point where the optical axis intersects the image plane. The image plane is located at distance \(f\) from the camera projection center along the optical axis, where \(f\) denotes the focal length. Before continuing, we must distinguish between:
  • image coordinates, which are affected by camera intrinsics such as focal length, principal point offset, as well as possibly pixel scaling and
  • normalized image coordinates, obtained after removing the camera intrinsic matrix \(K\)
The image coordinates are given by
  • \( \tilde{p}_I = \begin{pmatrix} u & v & 1 \end{pmatrix}^\top \)
  • \( \tilde{p}_I' = \begin{pmatrix} u' & v' & 1 \end{pmatrix}^\top \)
The normalized image coordinates are
  • \( \tilde{p}_N = K^{-1}\tilde{p}_I = \begin{pmatrix} x & y & 1 \end{pmatrix}^\top \)
  • \( \tilde{p}_N' = K'^{-1}\tilde{p}_I' = \begin{pmatrix} x' & y' & 1 \end{pmatrix}^\top \)
The difference is important as epipolar geometry fundamentally describes the geometric relationship between camera rays and the relative motion of the cameras, independent of the specific image sensor used. As established above pixel coordinates \((u,v)\) depend on camera intrinsics such as focal length, image resolution, principal point offset, and skew. Consequently, the same viewing ray can produce different pixel coordinates for different cameras. By transforming image coordinates into normalized image coordinates, these camera-specific effects are removed. The resulting coordinates describe only the viewing direction of the ray in the camera coordinate system.

The Epipolar Constraint

The two camera projection centers \(C\) and \(C'\), together with the world point \(P\), span the epipolar plane. Without loss of generality, I will choose the coordinate system of the left camera as the world coordinate system. Consequently, the left camera frame (\(L\)) has identity rotation and zero translation from the world frame (\(W\)):
  • \(R_{W \to L} = I\)
  • \(t_{W \to L} = (0,0,0)^\top\)
The relative pose of the right camera with respect to the left camera is therefore completely described by a rotation matrix \( R \in SO(3) \) and a translation vector \( t \in \mathbb{R}^3. \)
  • \(R_{W \to R} = R\)
  • \(t_{W \to R} = t\)
A world point \(P\) can be expressed in the coordinate systems of the left and right cameras as \(P\) and \(P'\), respectively. Thus the following relationship holds: \[ \begin{align*} P' &= RP + t \\[4pt] \lambda' \tilde{p}_N' &= R(\lambda \tilde{p}_N) + t && \textcolor{lightgray}{\left( \begin{array}{l} P = \lambda \tilde{p}_N \\ P' = \lambda' \tilde{p}_N' \end{array} \right)} \\[6pt] t \times (\lambda' \tilde{p}_N') &= t \times (\lambda R \tilde{p}_N + t) && \textcolor{lightgray}{\left( \texttt{Cross product from left} \right)} \\[6pt] \lambda' (t \times \tilde{p}_N') &= \lambda (t \times R\tilde{p}_N) + \underbrace{t \times t}_{=0} && \textcolor{lightgray}{\left( \texttt{Distributivity of cross product} \right)} \\[6pt] \lambda' \underbrace{\tilde{p}_N'^\top (t \times \tilde{p}_N')}_{=0} &= \lambda \tilde{p}_N'^\top (t \times R\tilde{p}_N) && \textcolor{lightgray}{\left( \texttt{Dot product from left} \right)} \\[6pt] 0 &= \lambda \tilde{p}_N'^\top (t_\times R \tilde{p}_N) && \textcolor{lightgray}{\left( t_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -t_z & t_y \\ t_z & 0 & -t_x \\ -t_y & t_x & 0 \end{bmatrix} \right)} \\[10pt] 0 &= \tilde{p}_N'^\top (t_\times R) \tilde{p}_N && \textcolor{lightgray}{\left( \lambda \neq 0 \right)} \\[6pt] 0 &= \tilde{p}_N'^\top E \tilde{p}_N && \textcolor{lightgray}{\left( \texttt{Essential matrix } E = t_\times R \right)} \end{align*} \] which establishes a correspondence between both image points through the so called Essential matrix \(E\).
