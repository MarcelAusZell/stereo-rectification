Epipolar Lines

Normalized Camera Coordinates

The essential matrix that we just derived operates on normalized camera coordinates rather than directly on image pixel coordinates. We can change that though. Our pixels
  • \( \tilde{p}_I = \begin{pmatrix} u & v & 1 \end{pmatrix}^\top \)
  • \( \tilde{p}_I' = \begin{pmatrix} u' & v' & 1 \end{pmatrix}^\top \)
contain camera intrinsic informations \(K\) and \(K'\) such as such as the focal length and principal point \[ K = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad K' = \begin{pmatrix} f_x' & 0 & c_x' \\ 0 & f_y' & c_y' \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \] Using these matrices, we can transform the image pixel coordinates into normalized camera coordinates:
  • \( \tilde{p}_N = K^{-1}\tilde{p}_I = \begin{pmatrix} x & y & 1 \end{pmatrix}^\top \)
  • \( \tilde{p}_N' = K'^{-1}\tilde{p}_I' = \begin{pmatrix} x' & y' & 1 \end{pmatrix}^\top \)
Substituting this into the epipolar constraint gives Substituting this into the epipolar constraint gives \[ \begin{align*} 0 &= \tilde{p}_N'^\top E \tilde{p}_N \\[4pt] &= \left(K'^{-1}\tilde{p}_I'\right)^\top E \left(K^{-1}\tilde{p}_I\right) && \textcolor{lightgray}{\left( \begin{array}{l} \tilde{p}_N = K^{-1}\tilde{p}_I \\ \tilde{p}_N' = K'^{-1}\tilde{p}_I' \end{array} \right)} \\[6pt] &= \tilde{p}_I'^\top K'^{-T} E K^{-1}\tilde{p}_I && \textcolor{lightgray}{\left( \texttt{Transpose rule } (AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top \right)} \\[6pt] &= \tilde{p}_I'^\top F\tilde{p}_I && \textcolor{lightgray}{\left( \texttt{Fundamental matrix } F = K'^{-T} E K^{-1} \right)} \end{align*} \] where \(F\) denotes the so called Fundamental Matrix. The epipolar constraint can now be written directly in image coordinates as \[ \tilde{p}_I'^\top F \tilde{p}_I = 0. \]

Epipolar Lines

Given a point \(\tilde{p}_I\) in the left image, the corresponding point in the right image is not arbitrary. Instead, it is constrained to lie on a specific line in the right image, called the epipolar line. To see why this represents a line, let us expand the expression explicitly. Given \[ F = \begin{pmatrix} f_{11} & f_{12} & f_{13} \\ f_{21} & f_{22} & f_{23} \\ f_{31} & f_{32} & f_{33} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \tilde{p}_I = \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \] we multiply the fundamental matrix with the image point: \[ F\tilde{p}_I = \begin{pmatrix} f_{11}u + f_{12}v + f_{13} \\ f_{21}u + f_{22}v + f_{23} \\ f_{31}u + f_{32}v + f_{33} \end{pmatrix} =: \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{pmatrix} =: l'. \] The epipolar constraint can therefore be written as \[ \begin{align*} \tilde{p}_I'^\top F \tilde{p}_I &= 0 \\ \tilde{p}_I'^\top l' &= 0 \\ \begin{pmatrix} u' & v' & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{pmatrix} &= 0 \\ au' + bv' + c &= 0. \end{align*} \] Rearranging gives \[ v' = -\frac{a}{b}u' - \frac{c}{b}. \] This has exactly the form of a standard 2D line equation \[ y = mx + d. \] Therefore, \(l' = F\tilde{p}_I\) represents a line in the right image, called the epipolar line corresponding to the point \(\tilde{p}_I\). Similarly, a point \(\tilde{p}_I'\) in the right image defines an epipolar line in the left image by \[ l = F^\top \tilde{p}_I'. \] Every valid correspondence point \(\tilde{p}_I'\) must therefore lie on the line \(l'\). This is important because, instead of searching for corresponding points over the entire image, the search only needs to be performed along the corresponding epipolar line.
