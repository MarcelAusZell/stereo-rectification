Rectification construction

Rectifiying Rotation

As established above the right camera pose relative to the left camera is \[P' = RP + t\] As such and in the convention that we have used this far (left camera frame coincides with world camera frame) the translation vector \(t\) points from the left camera to the right camera. Because we want to make disparity horizontal we choose this baseline vector (just normalized) as the rectified x-axis

\[r_x = \frac{t}{\|t\|}\]

From there we only need two more perpendicular unit vectors. A common choice is to preserve the orginal "up" direction as much as possible. So we can use it to construct the new rectifiying z-axis

\[r_z = \frac{r_x \times u_{\text{old}}}{\|r_x \times u_{\text{old}}\|}\]

With two orthornormal axis the third one is simply the cross product those two

\[r_y = r_z \times r_x\]

Putting it all together the rectifiying rotation is built from these axes \[R_{\text{rect}} = \begin{bmatrix} r_x^\top \\ r_y^\top \\ r_z^\top \end{bmatrix}\]

Rectifiying Intrinsics

After choosing the rectified rotation, we also need to decide how the rectified camera projects points back onto the image plane. This is described by a new intrinsic matrix \[ K_{\text{rect}} = \begin{bmatrix} f_x^{\text{rect}} & 0 & c_x^{\text{rect}} \\ 0 & f_y^{\text{rect}} & c_y^{\text{rect}} \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \] The important point is that both rectified cameras should use the same intrinsic matrix. This means that after rectification, both images are projected onto the same kind of virtual image plane. A simple choice is to use a shared focal length, for example the average of the original focal lengths: \[ f_x^{\text{rect}} = f_y^{\text{rect}} = \frac{f_x + f_y + f_x' + f_y'}{4} \] For now we can set the skew to zero and place the new principal point near the center of the rectified image: \[ c_x^{\text{rect}} = \frac{w}{2}, \qquad c_y^{\text{rect}} = \frac{h}{2} \] where \(w\) and \(h\) are the width and height of the rectified output image. So the rectified intrinsic matrix becomes \[ K_{\text{rect}} = \begin{bmatrix} f^{\text{rect}} & 0 & \frac{w}{2} \\ 0 & f^{\text{rect}} & \frac{h}{2} \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \] Again, this matrix does not come from a real camera. It defines a virtual camera that we use only for the rectified images. Together, \(R_{\text{rect}}\) and \(K_{\text{rect}}\) define where each original image point should move in the rectified image. For the left image, the rectifying homography is \[ H = K_{\text{rect}} R_{\text{rect}} K^{-1} \] and for the right image it is \[ H' = K_{\text{rect}} R_{\text{rect}} R^\top K'^{-1} \] The term \(K^{-1}\) first turns a pixel into a normalized camera ray. The rotation then expresses this ray in the rectified camera frame. Finally, \(K_{\text{rect}}\) projects the rotated ray back into pixel coordinates of the rectified image.
