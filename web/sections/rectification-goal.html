The Goal of Rectification

Why Rectification?

Searching along epipolar lines already reduces the correspondence problem from a two-dimensional search to a one-dimensional search. However, the epipolar lines may still be slanted and different for each image point. Rectification simplifies this further by warping both images with suitable homographies so that all corresponding epipolar lines become horizontal and aligned with the image rows. After rectification, for corresponding points \[ p_I = \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad p_I' = \begin{bmatrix} u' \\ v' \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \] we have \[ v = v'. \] Therefore, matching no longer requires searching along arbitrary epipolar lines. Instead, for a point in the left image, the matching point in the right image only needs to be searched along the same image row.

Geometric Intuition

The reason epipolar lines become horizontal after rectification can be understood geometrically. Originally, the two cameras may observe the scene from different orientations, causing the epipolar lines to appear slanted and irregular in the images. Rectification virtually rotates both cameras into a new common configuration where
  • both image planes are parallel
  • both cameras look in the same direction
  • the translation between the cameras is purely horizontal
In this virtual stereo setup, corresponding scene points naturally appear on the same image row. As a result, all epipolar lines become horizontal. Importantly, the physical cameras are not moved. Instead, both images are warped using homographies so that they appear as if they had been captured by this better-aligned virtual stereo pair.
